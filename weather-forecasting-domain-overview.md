# Weather Forecasting Domain Overview

Weather forecasting is the practice of predicting atmospheric conditions at specific places and times. Meteorologists collect observations from weather stations, satellites, and radar systems around the world. They use these observations to understand current weather patterns and apply scientific models to project how conditions will evolve. Forecasts are produced at different time scales—from the next few hours to weeks ahead—with accuracy generally decreasing as the forecast extends further into the future. When fresh observations aren't available, forecasters rely on the most recent predictions to provide approximate conditions.

## Domain Elements

**Location**: A specific place on Earth where weather is observed and forecasted, identified by name, coordinates, or postal code.

**Atmospheric Conditions**: The state of the air and weather at a given time and place, encompassing all measurable properties such as temperature, humidity, wind, pressure, precipitation, cloud cover, and visibility that together describe what the weather is like.

**Observation**: A recorded measurement of actual atmospheric conditions at a particular location and time, collected from instruments at weather stations, ships, aircraft, or satellites.

**Prediction**: An estimate of what weather conditions will be like at a future time and place, based on current observations and meteorological science.

**Hourly Prediction**: A forecast showing expected weather conditions for each hour, typically covering the next one to three days.

**Daily Prediction**: A forecast summarizing expected weather for an entire day, showing the range of conditions from morning to night, usually extending one to two weeks ahead.

**Fallback Prediction**: The best available weather estimate when current observations cannot be obtained, drawn from the most recent hourly or daily predictions that cover the time in question.

**Weather Alert**: An official notice issued by meteorological agencies warning the public about dangerous or severe weather approaching or occurring in their area.

**Precipitation**: Water in any form falling from clouds—rain, snow, sleet, freezing rain, or hail—measured by amount and intensity.

**Temperature**: The degree of heat in the air, reported as the actual value and the "apparent temperature" accounting for how wind or humidity makes it feel warmer or colder.

**Wind**: The movement of air across the surface, described by its speed and the direction from which it blows.

**Atmospheric Pressure**: The weight of air pressing down at a location, which influences weather patterns and is measured to track approaching systems.

**Humidity**: The amount of moisture in the air, affecting how comfortable conditions feel and the likelihood of precipitation.

**Visibility**: The distance at which objects can be clearly seen, reduced by fog, precipitation, dust, or smoke.

**Sky Condition**: A description of cloud cover ranging from clear skies to overcast, indicating how much sunlight reaches the surface.

**Radar Image**: A picture showing where precipitation is falling and how heavy it is, created by bouncing radio waves off water droplets and ice crystals in the atmosphere.

**Satellite Image**: A photograph taken from space showing cloud patterns, storm systems, and large-scale weather features across regions or continents.

**Air Quality**: A measure of how clean or polluted the air is, indicating whether breathing conditions are healthy or pose risks to sensitive groups.

**Ultraviolet Index**: A scale indicating the strength of sunburn-producing UV radiation reaching the ground, used to assess sun exposure risk.

**Weather Station**: A facility equipped with instruments to measure temperature, precipitation, wind, pressure, and other atmospheric conditions at ground level.

**Astronomical Information**: The times when the sun and moon rise and set, plus the current phase of the moon, relevant to daily light conditions.

**Severe Weather Event**: A documented occurrence of hazardous conditions—such as tornadoes, hurricanes, blizzards, or heat waves—with defined boundaries in time and space.
